## Chapter 1: Understand the loop

**Objective:** Tell the difference between thinking something through and going round in a loop.

**Time:** about 8 minutes

**Safety note:** If overthinking comes with low mood or anxiety that's affecting your sleep, work or relationships, please talk to a doctor or a mental health professional. findahelpline.com lists free, confidential support lines for your country.

### Understand the loop (intro, optional)

Overthinking isn't a flaw in you. It's your mind trying to make something uncertain feel certain before you act. In this chapter you'll learn to spot the moment useful thinking turns into a loop, sort some thoughts, and look at one of your own. Nothing here is scored.


### The loop (lesson, optional)

Some thinking moves you forward. You find a fact, weigh two options, notice what matters to you, and a next step appears. Other thinking goes round. The same question comes back, nothing new is added, and you end up more tired than when you started.

The loop usually begins with a question that has no answer yet. What if I choose wrong? What will they think? Your mind replays the situation and rehearses what might happen, looking for certainty. For a moment that feels like progress. Then the doubt returns, and the circle starts again.

Researchers who study repetitive thinking describe two styles. One is concrete and asks how: what exactly happened, and what could I do next? The other is abstract and asks why: why am I like this, why does this always happen? The first tends to help people solve problems. The second tends to keep them stuck.

Some uncertainty can't be removed by thinking. You can't know how a choice will turn out before you make it. When thinking stops adding anything, more of it won't bring the certainty you're looking for. That's the moment to notice the loop and step out of it.

Three questions help you tell which kind of thinking you're in. Is there new information here? Is there a next step I can take? Would thinking about this longer change what I decide? If all three answers are no, you're probably in the loop.


### Forward or round? (sort)

Six thoughts someone had while deciding whether to apply for a new job.

**Prompt:** Sort each one: does it move them forward, or go round?

| Thought | Suggested reading | Why |
|---|---|---|
| When does the application close? | Moves forward | A question with an answer. Once it's found, it's done. |
| What if they think I'm not good enough? | Goes round | Nobody can answer this before applying, so thinking about it longer won't settle it. |
| Which parts of the role would I actually enjoy? | Moves forward | It asks about you, and you can answer it. It adds information. |
| Why do I always do this to myself? | Goes round | An abstract why. It tends to bring a judgement, not a next step. |
| Who could I ask what the team is like? | Moves forward | It leads to an action that brings in something new. |
| But what if it all goes wrong anyway? | Goes round | The loop's favourite question. It comes back however often it's answered. |

**Afterwards:** The thoughts that move forward can be answered or acted on. The ones that go round ask for a certainty nobody has yet. Your sorting doesn't need to match this. What matters is noticing the difference.


### Your loop (rehearse)

**Prompt:** What's a question your mind keeps coming back to at the moment? Write it the way it sounds in your head.

**Example offered (never prefilled):** What if I've already left it too late to change careers?


### The three questions (reflect)

**Prompt:** Look at what you wrote. Is there new information in it, a next step, or anything more thinking would change? Write what you notice.

**Example offered (never prefilled):** No new information. The one step I can take is to look at two courses this week.


### Try it for real (plan, optional)

**Prompt:** If you want: the next time the loop starts, name it quietly ("loop") and ask the three questions. What will you do, and when?

**Example offered (never prefilled):** Tonight, if I start replaying the meeting: say "loop" and ask the three questions.

## Chapter 2: Separate facts from assumptions

**Objective:** Separate what you know from what you're guessing before you decide.

**Time:** about 8 minutes

**Safety note:** If overthinking comes with low mood or anxiety that's affecting your sleep, work or relationships, please talk to a doctor or a mental health professional. findahelpline.com lists free, confidential support lines for your country.

### Separate facts from assumptions (intro, optional)

Decisions feel heavier than they need to when guesses pass for facts. In this chapter you'll learn a simple split, practise it on someone else's decision, then use it on one of yours. Nothing here is scored.


### What you know, and what you're guessing (lesson, optional)

When a decision has been going round for a while, facts and guesses blur together. "The job pays more" and "I'd hate it there" start to feel equally true, though only one of them can be checked.

A fact is something you could check, or that someone else would agree happened: a date, a number, something that was said. An assumption is a prediction, a guess at what someone else thinks, or a story about what something means. Assumptions aren't bad. You need them to decide anything. They just aren't known yet.

Splitting the two doesn't make the decision for you. It shows you which assumptions you could test, even a little, and which part of the decision is a real trade-off that no amount of thinking will remove.

Often one assumption carries most of the weight. "My manager would see it as disloyal." "I won't make friends there." Finding that one, and asking how you could check it, is usually worth more than another night of turning the whole thing over.


### Fact or assumption? (sort)

Maya is deciding whether to move to another city for a job. This is what's on her mind.

**Prompt:** Sort each one: something she knows, or something she's guessing?

| Thought | Suggested reading | Why |
|---|---|---|
| The new job pays 15% more. | Fact | She has the offer in writing. It can be checked. |
| I'd be lonely there. | Assumption | A prediction. It might be true, but it isn't known yet. She could find out who she already knows in that city. |
| My lease ends in March. | Fact | A date in a contract. |
| My family would feel I'd abandoned them. | Assumption | A guess at what other people feel. She could ask them. |
| The office is a 20-minute walk from the flat I viewed. | Fact | Measurable, and she's measured it. |
| I won't be good at the new work. | Assumption | A prediction about herself. She could look at what the role involves, or talk to someone who does it. |

**Afterwards:** Half of what's on her mind can be checked, and it's mostly settled. The other half, the part that feels heaviest, is guesses about loneliness, family and ability. That doesn't make the guesses wrong. It means they're where she could find out more.


### Your decision (rehearse)

**Prompt:** Name a decision you've been turning over. Then write the facts: only what you know, or could check.

**Example offered (never prefilled):** Whether to take the team-lead role. Facts: it starts in May, it's a 10% raise, I'd manage four people.


### The heaviest guess (reflect)

**Prompt:** Now the assumptions. Which guess is carrying most of the weight, and how could you check it, even a little?

**Example offered (never prefilled):** That I'd be bad at managing people. I could ask Sam, who led the team last year, what it's really like.


### Test one assumption (plan, optional)

**Prompt:** If you want, choose one small way to check that guess, and when you'll do it.

**Example offered (never prefilled):** Ask Sam for twenty minutes on Thursday.

## Chapter 3: Practise a pause

**Objective:** Put a little space between a racing thought and what you do next.

**Time:** about 7 minutes

**Safety note:** If overthinking comes with low mood or anxiety that's affecting your sleep, work or relationships, please talk to a doctor or a mental health professional. findahelpline.com lists free, confidential support lines for your country.

### Practise a pause (intro, optional)

When the loop speeds up, thoughts arrive faster than you can look at them. In this chapter you'll learn why a short pause helps, try a one-minute pause, and choose when you might use it. Nothing here is scored, and you can stop the pause at any time.


### Space between the thought and you (lesson, optional)

A pause doesn't stop thoughts, and it isn't meant to. It puts a little space between a thought and what you do next, so the thought stops deciding for you.

Attention is something you can move. Right now it might be on a worry. You can move it to your breathing, then to what you can see, then to what you can hear. Moving it on purpose, even for a minute, is practice in stepping out of the loop.

Many people find that breathing out a little more slowly than they breathe in helps them settle. If focusing on your breath feels uncomfortable, skip that part and use what you can see and hear instead.

The thought may still be there afterwards. That's fine. The aim isn't to get rid of it. It's to come back to it on purpose, at a time you choose, rather than because it pulled you there.


### A one-minute pause (pause, optional)

Find a position that's comfortable. You can keep your eyes open. Follow the words as they change, or read them all at your own pace.

| Cue | Seconds |
|---|---|
| Notice the thought that's been loudest today. Name it quietly: "loop". | 10 |
| Breathe out slowly, a little longer than you breathe in. | 12 |
| Again. Out a little longer than in. | 10 |
| Look around. Find three things you can see. | 10 |
| Listen. Find two things you can hear. | 10 |
| Come back. Is there one next step, or a time you'll return to the thought? | 8 |


### When the loop speeds up (choice)

It's late. You're replaying a conversation from today, and it's getting louder. What do you do?

| Option | What tends to happen | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Keep going over it until it's sorted | Often it isn't sorted, and sleep gets later. | Late at night there's rarely new information, so the same question keeps coming back without an answer. |
| Pick up your phone to take your mind off it | It can work for a while, and the thought is often waiting when the phone goes down. | Distraction moves attention away without choosing a time to come back, so the loop stays open. |
| Pause, write one line about it, and set a time to come back | The thought tends to loosen its grip, because it has a place to go. | Naming it and choosing when to return tells your mind it won't be forgotten, so it has less reason to keep reminding you. |


### After the pause (reflect)

**Prompt:** What did you notice during the pause, or afterwards? The thought might be quieter, the same, or louder. Any of these is fine to write.

**Example offered (never prefilled):** Quieter for a minute, then it came back, but less urgent.


### Try it for real (plan, optional)

**Prompt:** If you want, choose one moment this week to try a one-minute pause. What will you do, and when?

**Example offered (never prefilled):** When I notice I'm replaying a meeting: one-minute pause, then one line in my notes.


### Return to Earth (close, optional)

You've practised putting space between a thought and what you do next. Next: Chapter 4, Enter the Decision Lab. Your pocket card, The One-Minute Pause, stays in this chapter whenever you want it.

## Chapter 4: Enter the Decision Lab

**Objective:** Widen a stuck decision into real options, and see what each one costs.

**Time:** about 10 minutes

**Safety note:** If overthinking comes with low mood or anxiety that's affecting your sleep, work or relationships, please talk to a doctor or a mental health professional. findahelpline.com lists free, confidential support lines for your country.

### Enter the Decision Lab (intro, optional)

In the Decision Lab you'll widen a stuck decision into real options, see what each one costs, and notice which choices are easy to undo. Then you'll lay out one of your own decisions, without adding anything up. Nothing here is scored.


### More than two doors (lesson, optional)

Overthinking often squeezes a decision down to two choices: do it, or don't. Stay or go. With only two doors, each one looks risky, and the mind swings back and forth between them.

Most decisions have more than two options. Two are often missing: a smaller version (try it for a month, ask for a trial, start part-time) and not yet (wait until a date, or a piece of information, that you name). Adding them doesn't make the decision for you. It often makes it lighter.

Every option has a cost, including doing nothing. Looking for the option with no downside is one of the ways the loop keeps going. A more useful question is: which cost am I willing to pay, for what matters most to me?

It also helps to ask how easy a choice would be to undo. Many choices can be reversed, and for those, trying teaches you more than thinking does. Save your careful weighing for the few that are hard to undo.


### Easy or hard to undo? (sort)

Six choices people often think over for a long time.

**Prompt:** Sort each one: easy to undo, or hard to undo?

| Thought | Suggested reading | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Trying a new class at the gym for a month | Easy to undo | You can stop after a month, and you'll know more than you do now. |
| Signing a two-year lease | Hard to undo | Leaving early usually costs money and time. |
| Asking for a trial week on another team | Easy to undo | If it doesn't fit, you go back. You've spent a week and learned a lot. |
| Resigning before you have another job | Hard to undo | It can be undone, but not easily, and not on your terms. |
| Taking an evening course in something you're curious about | Easy to undo | If it isn't for you, you stop. The cost is a few evenings. |
| Selling the car you rely on for work | Hard to undo | Replacing it usually costs more than you got, and you need it in the meantime. |

**Afterwards:** Easy-to-undo choices usually deserve less thinking than they get: you'll learn more by trying. Save careful thinking for the few that are hard to undo, and even then, look for a smaller version you could try first.


### Your Decision Lab (lab)

Take a decision you've been turning over. It can be the one from Chapter 2.

**Prompt:** Write two or three options. Try to include a smaller version, or "not yet, until…". Then name what matters most to you in this decision.

**Afterwards:** There's no total here, on purpose. Look at the pattern instead: is there an option that helps with what matters most, at a cost you could live with? Notice whether the smaller version or "not yet" changes the picture.

**Example offered (never prefilled):** Take the team-lead role / Ask for a three-month trial of the role / Not yet: decide after the April review / Time with my family / Learning something new


### What stands out (reflect)

**Prompt:** Look at your lab. What stands out? An option you hadn't really considered, or a cost you'd be willing to pay?

**Example offered (never prefilled):** The trial helps with both things that matter. The cost is waiting a little longer to know.


### One small step (plan, optional)

**Prompt:** If you want, choose one small step toward an option, or to find something out, and when.

**Example offered (never prefilled):** Email the team lead on Monday to ask about a trial.
